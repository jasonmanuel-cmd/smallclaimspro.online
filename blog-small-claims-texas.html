How to File Small Claims Court in Texas — Complete 2026 Guide

What Is Texas Small Claims Court?

Texas doesn't use the term "small claims court" officially — the venue is the Justice of the Peace (JP) Court, also called Justice Court. Every county in Texas is divided into precincts, and each precinct has a JP court. Small claims cases in Texas are governed by the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure and Tex. Gov't Code §27.031.

Unlike California, attorneys are permitted to appear in Texas JP courts, but the vast majority of small claims cases are self-represented. The rules are designed for non-lawyers, and judges are used to hearing cases from everyday Texans without legal training.

Where to File in Texas

File in the Justice Court for the precinct where the defendant resides or where the incident occurred. Texas has 835 JP courts across 254 counties. Find yours at txcourts.gov.

Claim Limits and Statute of Limitations

Texas has one of the most generous small claims systems in the U.S.:

CategoryLimit / PeriodStatute
Maximum claim$20,000Tex. Gov't Code §27.031
Written contract4 yearsTex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §16.004
Oral contract4 years§16.004
Property damage4 years§16.004
Personal injury2 years§16.003

Most contract and property claims have a generous 4-year window. Personal injury claims — like a car accident or slip and fall — must be filed within 2 years of the incident.

SOL Warning

The clock starts on the date the wrong occurred, not when you discovered it (with limited exceptions). If you're close to 4 years, file now. Missing the SOL means your claim is permanently time-barred.

Step 1 — Send a Demand Letter Before Filing

Texas law does not require a demand letter before filing in JP court, but sending one is almost always worth it for two reasons:

  1. It often works. A formal certified letter making clear you intend to sue prompts payment in a surprising number of cases — especially for contractor disputes and unpaid invoices.
  2. It strengthens your case. Texas judges expect to see that you tried to resolve the dispute first. A demand letter with a certified mail receipt shows good faith and makes you look credible.

What your Texas demand letter should include:

  • Exact amount owed with a breakdown
  • How the debt arose (contract, invoice, agreement, etc.)
  • A 10-day deadline for payment
  • A statement that you will file in Justice Court if not paid
  • Your full name, mailing address, and phone number
Texas-Specific Tip

For contractor disputes under the Texas Residential Construction Liability Act (RCLA) or home repair contracts, there are additional notice requirements before filing. Step 2 — File Your Petition at JP Court

1

Get the right form

Ask your local JP court for the small claims petition form — often called "Original Petition – Small Claims" (Form SC-1 or a local equivalent). Many JP courts have their own version. You can also find forms at txcourts.gov or your county court's website.

2

Complete the petition

Fill in your full legal name and address (plaintiff), the defendant's full legal name and address, the exact dollar amount you're claiming, and a concise explanation of what happened and why the defendant owes you money. Keep the description factual and specific — dates, amounts, and what was promised or agreed to.

3

File at the JP court clerk's window

Bring 2 copies of your completed petition (one for you, one for the court). Pay the filing fee (see table below). The clerk will stamp your copy, assign a case number, and schedule a hearing date — typically 2–6 weeks out.

Texas Small Claims Filing Fees

Filing fees vary by precinct and claim amount but are generally in this range:

Claim AmountApproximate Fee
Up to $1,000$46 – $55
$1,001 – $5,000$55 – $70
$5,001 – $10,000$70 – $85
$10,001 – $20,000$85 – $100

You may also pay a separate service fee (typically $75–$100) for the constable or sheriff to serve the defendant. Some JP courts allow certified mail service for lower amounts, which costs significantly less.

Step 3 — Serve the Defendant

After you file, the JP court will issue a citation — the official notice ordering the defendant to appear. Texas requires formal service; you cannot simply mail it yourself in most cases.

Service options in Texas:

  • Constable or sheriff service — the most common and most reliable method. Pay the fee (usually $75–$100) at the clerk's window. The constable delivers the citation to the defendant personally.
  • Certified mail — some JP courts allow this for lower-value claims. The clerk handles it and keeps the return receipt.
  • Process server — a licensed process server can also serve the citation if ordered by the court.
Important

If the defendant cannot be served at the address you provided, your case will be delayed. Before filing, make sure you have the defendant's current home address or business address. If it's a business, you may need the registered agent's address from the Texas Secretary of State.

Step 4 — The Hearing

Texas JP Court hearings are informal compared to district court, but they are real legal proceedings. The judge will hear both sides and ask questions. Here's what to bring:

  • Your demand letter and the certified mail receipt (proof you tried to resolve it)
  • Any contracts, invoices, receipts, or estimates
  • Text messages, emails, or other written communications with the defendant
  • Photos of damaged property, incomplete work, or defective goods
  • Any witnesses who can support your version of events
  • A clear, written timeline of events — judges appreciate organized plaintiffs

The defendant will also have a chance to respond. Texas allows the defendant to file a counterclaim at or before the hearing, so be prepared for that possibility.

Hearing Day Tip

Arrive early, dress professionally, and speak directly to the judge. Say "Your Honor." Stick to the facts — dates, amounts, and what was agreed to. Judges are not moved by emotion; they're moved by documentation.

After the Judgment — Collecting Your Money

Winning a judgment is step one. Collecting it is step two — and Texas gives you several tools to enforce a judgment:

  • Writ of Execution — allows a constable or sheriff to seize and sell the defendant's non-exempt property to satisfy the judgment
  • Wage garnishment — Texas limits wage garnishment to specific circumstances (child support, student loans), but you can garnish bank accounts
  • Abstract of Judgment — file with the county clerk to create a lien on the defendant's real property in that county
  • Post-judgment discovery — you can require the defendant to disclose their assets under oath

Texas judgment liens last 10 years and can be renewed. If the defendant has real property in Texas, an abstract of judgment ensures you'll be paid when they sell or refinance.
