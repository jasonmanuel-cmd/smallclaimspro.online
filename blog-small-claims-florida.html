Florida Small Claims Court — The Basics

Small claims in Florida are handled by the County Court in each of Florida's 67 counties. Cases are governed by the Florida Small Claims Rules (Fla. R. Sm. Cl. 7.010–7.350). The procedures are intentionally simple, and judges frequently assist unrepresented parties in navigating the process.

Attorneys are permitted to appear, but most plaintiffs and defendants represent themselves. Florida's small claims system has one notable requirement that sets it apart from most states: mandatory pre-trial mediation. If your case doesn't settle at mediation, it proceeds to trial — but mediation often leads to voluntary resolution without needing a judge's ruling.

Where to File in Florida

File in the County Court for the county where the defendant resides, has a principal place of business, or where the contract was to be performed or the cause of action arose. Find your county clerk at flcourts.gov.

Florida's 5-year statute of limitations for written contracts is longer than most states — but don't let that lull you into waiting. Evidence disappears, witnesses forget, and defendants move. File as soon as you've documented your case clearly.

Before Filing — Send a Demand Letter

Florida law doesn't require a demand letter before filing in small claims court, but experienced Florida judges consistently give more weight to plaintiffs who documented a genuine attempt at resolution. A proper demand letter:

  • Shows the judge you acted in good faith
  • Establishes a paper trail of the debt and the defendant's refusal to pay
  • Often resolves the dispute without any court involvement
  • Can support a claim for attorney's fees or costs under certain Florida statutes (e.g., Fla. Stat. §57.105 for frivolous defenses)

Your Florida demand letter should include:

  • The specific amount owed and an itemized breakdown
  • The factual basis (what happened, when, and what was agreed)
  • A clear 10–14 day deadline for payment
  • Notice that you will file in County Court if not resolved
  • Your contact information and signature

How to File Your Statement of Claim

1

Get the Statement of Claim form

Visit your county clerk's office or download the Statement of Claim form from your county court's website. The form varies by county — some counties have specific versions. The Florida Courts E-Filing Portal (myflcourtaccess.com) allows online filing in many counties.

2

Complete the form carefully

List your full legal name and address, the defendant's full legal name and current address, the exact dollar amount claimed, and a clear, factual statement of why the defendant owes you the money. Keep it factual — no emotional language. Attach a copy of any relevant contract, invoice, or agreement.

3

File and pay the fee

File in person at the county clerk's office or online via the e-filing portal. Pay the filing fee (see below). You'll receive a stamped copy and a scheduled date for the pre-trial conference or mediation session.

Florida's filing fees are higher than most states, particularly for larger claims:

Additional fees apply for service of process (typically $40–$80 per defendant for sheriff service). Some counties also charge a small surcharge for the mediation program.

Fee Waiver Available

If you cannot afford the filing fee, Florida courts allow you to request a fee waiver by filing a Determination of Civil Indigent Status (Form AOSC 09-30). Approval is based on income guidelines.

Serving the Defendant in Florida

After you file, the clerk will issue a summons. Florida requires formal service of process — you cannot serve the defendant yourself.

Service options:

  • Sheriff service — most common. Pay the sheriff's office fee (usually $40–$80) to have a deputy deliver the summons to the defendant.
  • Certified process server — Florida has a list of certified process servers who can serve summons. Often faster than the sheriff in busy counties like Miami-Dade or Broward.
  • Certified mail — Florida does not permit certified mail for initial service of process in small claims. It must be personal service.
Serving a Business in Florida

If the defendant is a Florida LLC or corporation, you must serve the registered agent. Look up the registered agent at search.sunbiz.org (Florida Division of Corporations). Serving the wrong person voids the service.

Pre-Trial Mediation — Florida's Unique Requirement

Florida-Specific Requirement

Mediation is required before most Florida small claims trials.

Under Florida Small Claims Rule 7.090, the court will order both parties to attend a pre-trial conference. At that conference, the judge may refer the case to mediation. Mediation is typically conducted by a court-certified mediator and lasts 1–3 hours. If you reach an agreement, it becomes a binding court order. If mediation fails, the case proceeds to trial.

How to prepare for Florida mediation:

  • Bring all documentation — contracts, invoices, texts, photos
  • Know your bottom line: the minimum you would accept to settle
  • Be prepared to negotiate — mediation is a negotiation, not a hearing
  • The mediator cannot force either party to settle; their job is to facilitate
  • If you reach a settlement, get it in writing before leaving
Mediation Strategy

Many defendants offer partial payment at mediation hoping to close the matter. If the partial offer is reasonable and you factor in the time cost of going to trial, consider it. A bird in hand at mediation is worth more than a judgment you may never collect.

The Trial

If mediation fails, the case proceeds to trial — usually scheduled 4–8 weeks after the mediation date. Florida small claims trials are bench trials (a judge decides, no jury). The Florida Small Claims Rules allow a more relaxed evidentiary standard than regular civil court, but you still need to present your case clearly.

What to bring to your Florida small claims trial:

  • Your demand letter with certified mail receipt
  • The original contract, invoice, or agreement
  • All relevant correspondence (text messages, emails, letters)
  • Photos documenting the issue (damaged goods, incomplete work, etc.)
  • Any witnesses — notify them well in advance of the trial date
  • A clear 1-page summary of your claim: what happened, when, and how much you're owed

Collecting Your Florida Judgment

Once you have a judgment, the defendant is legally required to pay. But collection requires you to take action:

  • Writ of Execution — authorizes the sheriff to seize and sell the defendant's non-exempt property
  • Wage garnishment — Florida allows wage garnishment for judgment creditors (limited to 25% of disposable earnings under federal law, with Florida head-of-family exemptions)
  • Bank account levy — you can garnish a bank account; Florida has limited exemptions for this
  • Judgment lien — record a certified copy of the judgment with the county clerk to create a lien on real property

Florida judgments are good for 20 years and can be renewed. You can also request the defendant complete a Fact Information Sheet disclosing their assets and income.
